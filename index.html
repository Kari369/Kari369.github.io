In areas too vast or physically impossible for humans to reach, how can robots serve as extensions of human perception, not through the simple execution of commands, but through synchronized intent weighted against other factors?

I'm a student at The Knowledge Society, on a gap year developing my theoretical framework, Human–Machine Intent Synchronization (HMIS), while building the technical foundations needed to explore it.

Why it matters to me

Extending reach, without losing the human

I've always been interested in the relationship between what humans can perceive, what we can physically accomplish, and what technology could help us extend.

There are environments humans cannot safely enter, distances we cannot easily cover, and situations where our physical limitations restrict what we can observe or do. Robotics offers a way to extend our reach into these spaces. But I find myself interested in a question beyond simply making machines perform tasks: how can we make them understand what a human is trying to achieve, while accounting for the circumstances surrounding that intention?

This question is what led me to Human–Machine Intent Synchronization (HMIS). For most of 2026 that meant reading and writing. Since September it has meant building and testing in simulation.

Completed · Simulation

HMIS Experiment 1: Pilot A

Can HMIS tell deliberate, incidental and ambiguous operator input apart, using only observable signals?

The first experiment built from my framework. A simulated rover receives a stream of operator signals (hand position, muscle activation, facing) and has to decide whether a movement was meant as a command, was accidental, or is unclear and needs a question back. The true answer is hidden from the system until after it decides.

My part
Designed the experiment: the definitions of deliberate, incidental and ambiguous input, the scenarios, the confidence bands and the rules for changing them. Developed with guidance from my technical mentor.
Result
30 trials. The clear command and the half-finished halt were classified correctly 10/10. The accidental face-wipe was read as "uncertain" 10/10, so the robot asked instead of carrying on. I traced why: the score added up shape alone, with no sign the movement was meant for the robot. A proposed fix is recorded but not adopted until it is tested on new cases.
How it was made
Webots and Python. The code was written with AI assistance from my design; every rule change is logged with the evidence behind it.

Completed · Simulation

Policy-blending shared control

Can a robot infer which goal its driver is heading for and help, without taking over? A simplified adaptation of Dragan & Srinivasa's policy-blending formalism (2013).

What I did
Built a C controller for a simulated Clearpath Moose and ran 15 logged trials under a 0.4 s command delay.
Result
Median 7.65 s with assistance vs 10.27 s manual. Constant assistance with no goal inference was slightly faster still: knowing the goal and helping well are separate problems.

Early prototype · Browser

HMIS mission console

A browser demo of the core loop: an operator types an intent, the engine checks it against simulated field conditions, then executes, holds for confirmation, or flags a hazard.

How it was made
Built with a free Claude model for The Knowledge Society's Prompt-to-Product challenge. The engine is deterministic and narrow: typed input only, no real sensors.

Theoretical framework

Human–Machine Intent Synchronization

An independent investigation into how a machine can synchronize with what its operator is trying to achieve, instead of relying only on explicit commands. It is a theoretical framework, not a built system; the experiments above test it one piece at a time.

Questions it asks
What is the human trying to achieve? How should the machine handle uncertainty before it acts? How does synchronization continue as a loop of capture, act and observe? Where would it matter: mining, disaster response and infrastructure work.
Written so far
Two technical abstracts: one on intent synchronization for field deployment, one on machine behavior under uncertainty. Both are working documents, not validated results.
