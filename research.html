Research · Human–Machine Intent Synchronization

The questions, frameworks and ideas behind HMIS

HMIS began as an independent investigation into how machines could move beyond explicit commands and instead synchronize with the intent of the human operating them. This page presents the research direction and the technical work developed from that question.

Theory

HMIS is a developing framework. Its proposed architecture, operating principles and synchronization mechanisms are still being refined through research and experimentation.

Built

Software simulations that test selected pieces of it, described below. They are partial implementations, not a complete HMIS system, and they do not validate the whole framework.

Research direction

From commands to intent

The central investigation is not simply how to make machines more autonomous, but how to create a reliable relationship between human intent, machine interpretation, environmental context and action.

  1. What is the human actually trying to achieve?

    HMIS treats observable behavior as a signal from which intended outcomes may be inferred, rather than assuming that every action must be explicitly translated into a command.

  2. How should the machine respond to uncertainty?

    An intent prediction is not automatically correct. The framework therefore considers confidence, environmental constraints, validation, confirmation and safe degradation before execution.

  3. How does synchronization happen continuously?

    The relationship is a loop rather than a one-time command: capture, synchronize, act, observe the result, and feed that information into the next decision.

  4. Where could this matter in the real world?

    Environments where direct human presence is difficult or dangerous: field deployment, mining, disaster response and other infrastructure-level operations.

Technical abstracts

How the research is currently framed

These are working documents, not claims that the complete system has been experimentally validated.

Abstract 01

Human–Machine Intent Synchronization for Field Deployment Operations

Synchronizing human intent with machine behavior under environmental and operational constraints.

A human–machine interaction model in which the human remains the primary source of intent, while the machine interprets, predicts, validates and executes within defined operational boundaries.

Instead of a sequence of isolated commands, intent is treated as a continuous signal. The machine becomes a responsive teammate whose behavior is shaped by both the operator's intent and the state of the surrounding environment.

The framework focuses on field deployment, where an operator may need to influence a remote machine without entering the environment the machine is working in.

Status: a theoretical framework and ongoing design investigation. Prototyping, simulation and empirical validation are needed to establish performance.

Abstract 02

Intent Synchronization and Machine Behavior Under Uncertainty

Prediction, validation, feedback and graceful degradation when the machine's interpretation may be wrong.

A machine's reading of what a human intends can be uncertain, so this abstract looks at what should happen between prediction and execution.

HMIS proposes that the machine weighs its confidence and the operational context before committing to an action. Depending on the situation, it may execute within safe limits, ask for confirmation, or degrade to a safer state.

The consequences of each action then become information for the next synchronization cycle.

From description to testing

Experiments built so far

The abstracts ended with one next step: turn the concepts into simulations that test the proposed behavior. These are the first two.

Completed · Simulation

HMIS Experiment 1: Pilot A

Tests abstract 02's core question: can the machine tell a deliberate command from an accidental movement, and ask when it is unsure? 30 trials in Webots. Clear and half-finished commands were handled correctly; an accidental face-wipe was read as uncertain, and the cause was traced.

Completed · Simulation

Policy-blending shared control

A simplified adaptation of an existing shared-control method: the robot infers which goal its driver is heading for and helps without taking over. 15 logged trials under a 0.4 s command delay.

Research process

How the investigation is developing

  1. Observe

    Identify problems in existing human–machine interaction and where command-based systems become limiting.

  2. Frame

    Turn observations into technical questions, system behavior requirements and possible interaction models.

  3. Compare

    Connect the framework to existing research in human–computer interaction, robotics, shared autonomy and machine learning.

  4. Validate

    Move from conceptual architecture to simulations, prototypes and, eventually, real-world testing.

Selected literature

Research the framework is connected to

Points of comparison for HMIS, and what each one changed in my thinking.

  1. 1960

    Man–Computer Symbiosis

    J.C.R. Licklider

    An early vision of close cooperation between humans and computers, each doing what it does best. It is the conceptual predecessor of HMIS; my framework asks what that partnership looks like away from the desk, in the field.

  2. Shared autonomy

    Probabilistic Human Intent Recognition for Shared Autonomy in Assistive Robotics

    Jain and Argall

    A robot keeps several hypotheses about a person's goal and updates them as it observes. It showed me that intent recognition is one part of synchronization, not the whole of it.

  3. Search and rescue

    Human–Robot Team Performance Compared to Full Robot Autonomy in 16 Real-World Search and Rescue Missions

    An adaptation of the DARPA Subterranean Challenge

    Evidence from real missions that human involvement matters most when robots meet conditions they cannot handle alone, and that removing the human entirely leaves a system unable to recover.
